## 2. Our gap

1. **Neutral:** serves any reactor maker, class society and owner. The consortium and Core Power each serve their own projects.
2. **Repeatable:** one scorecard across ports, so each port adds to a network an owner can plan routes on. Today each study is one off.
3. **Applied, not general:** turns guidance into the specific list for one ship, one route and one port.
4. **One record:** the same evidence serves the port, the regulator and the insurer, and later carries the monitoring data (idea 2).

## 3. Where we lose

- The consortium, NEMO or the IAEA publishes a free port tool or template.
- Core Power, the largest project, keeps all readiness work in house.
- Class societies bundle port readiness into design approval.
- Ships slip past 2033 and studies dry up.
- Buyers trust a law firm or class society over a startup without nuclear credentials.

## 4. Strategy

Use the public guidance as our source and cite it; sell the application to one ship, route and port. Start with our contact and projects outside Core Power (Korean consortia, Hyundai Glovis and Liberty Global Logistics, Deployable Energy and Hornbeck, Bluecore) [L7 to L10]. Offer ports a scorecard that MARAD can reuse. Recruit a former regulator as an advisor to close the credentials gap.

## 5. Stress test: new, or better? (2026-10-07)

**Improved, not new.** Port readiness work exists as studies (Rotterdam, Corpus Christi) and guidance (Lloyd's Register, NEMO, IAEA) [L5, L6, L14, L17, L19]. No software product found [13], and the AI licensing tools serve land reactors [6 to 9]. What stays open: a neutral, repeatable port and route tool that turns guidance into a checkable record. **Value lever to test:** whether a shared record is worth a yearly fee when every party already pays its own advisors.
